AI agents can delegate work faster than humans can inspect the resulting organization.
This creates a control problem: individually bounded agents may collectively exceed a
shared budget, inherit stale authority, or continue acting while a concern awaits review.
We introduce execute as a working term for effective action capacity and specify an
institutional protocol for allocating it across organizations of agents. The protocol
combines attenuated grants, shared accounting, transitive revocation and direct review
access. An offline simulator evaluates eight authored scenarios across four control
regimes, three response delays and two delegation depths, yielding 192 deterministic
configurations. Logging and individual limits admit \LogViolations{} and
\LocalViolations{} out-of-mandate effects, respectively; hierarchical controls admit
none under complete mediation. Adding scripted review raises valid work from
\HierarchyCompleted{} to \ReviewCompleted{} of 348 opportunities, while causing
\ReviewFalseBlocks{} valid-work blocks after false complaints. A separate bypass
challenge defeats the broker's protection. These are consequences of explicit rules,
not evidence that representation changes model behavior or would have prevented a
historical incident. The contribution is a reproducible acceptance-test artifact and an
operational governance specification that connects organizational authority to effects,
while exposing the costs and limits of that connection.
